Contrarian or Retrieved?

Nine frontier AI models were asked for their most heretical beliefs. This page reads all answers and asks a simpler question than the statistics did: is any of this actually contrarian?

Mostly familiar ideas in bold packaging

On of answers, specialists in the field already agree with the “contrarian” claim.

Asked for a truth “very few people agree with”, the typical answer is one that of specialists would accept and only of ordinary people would. That combination is the whole story: ideas that startle a general audience and bore an expert. Nearly two thirds trace back to a named thinker — Nisbett & Wilson, Barry Schwartz, Peter Thiel.

Only are both unfamiliar to specialists and untraceable to a known source, and just would cost the speaker anything to say out loud.

The questions

What we actually asked

We asked the same thing seven different ways, because how you word the question turns out to matter more than which model you ask. Every chart and table below refers to these by their short code. Each was sent on its own, with no conversation before it, followed by one shared instruction: “Answer in 2-3 sentences. State the belief plainly.”

The Thiel loop

The main question we asked — “What important truth do very few people agree with you on?” — is Peter Thiel's signature interview question. The graders traced answers ( of everything) back to Thiel himself.

Not only the monopoly argument from Zero to One — also his technological-stagnation thesis, and the one that makes the point on its own: that contrarian thinking is what produces success. Asked for a contrarian belief, the model replies that being contrarian works.

Method & limits

How the grading works, and where it is soft

Three models from three labs (Gemini 3.7 Flash, GPT-5.6 Luna, DeepSeek V4 Pro) graded every answer independently at temperature 0 under one fixed rubric. Scores combine by median, so no single lab can move an answer's grade, and “traceable to a named thinker” needs two of the three to agree. Only answers graded by all three count.

The soft spot. “Specialists who would agree” is the graders' estimate, not a survey of actual specialists. It inherits whatever these models believe about expert opinion, which makes the absolute percentages softer than they look. What it does support is the comparison: the same graders, the same rubric, applied to every answer, so the ranking between prompts and between models is meaningful even where the absolute level is not.

One grader needed repairing. DeepSeek spent its whole token budget thinking and cut off its answer on 18% of claims — a failure concentrated in one grader, which would have quietly thinned the panel on whichever claims it found hardest. Those were re-graded with a larger budget rather than dropped.
